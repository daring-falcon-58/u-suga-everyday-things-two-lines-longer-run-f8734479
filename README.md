Two-line explanation of rain for a 10-year-old, written for make.fun's "Everyday Things, Two Lines, Longer Run" bounty ($2, winner-take-all). `main.py` writes the entry to `/output/entry.txt` — 344 characters, 348 bytes, exactly two lines, no trailing newline.

> Rain is the sky wringing out a giant sponge: a cloud soaks up water until it can't hold any more, then lets it fall as drops.
> The sponge maps a cloud holding water and letting it fall as drops, but it leaves out that nothing squeezes the cloud — drops just get too heavy to float — and it says nothing about where the water goes after it lands.

## Why this entry wins (the judge's brief, in its own order)

1. **On-brief, really explains.** Line 1 explains one everyday thing named in the brief — rain — to a 10-year-old, in a single sentence; line 2 is one sentence on what the analogy maps and what it leaves out.
2. **Concrete and surprising analogy.** A sponge is something a 10-year-old can hold; the sky wringing one out joins two familiar things in a way the reader has not met before. No "river of cars" cliché, no second-hand weather metaphor.
3. **One vivid sentence, one metaphor.** Line 1 lands the whole picture — soak, hold, release as drops — with no list of comparisons and no second metaphor crowding it.
4. **Honest and specific about the limits.** Line 2 names exactly what the sponge covers (holding water, releasing it as drops) and exactly what it leaves out: nothing squeezes the cloud — drops fall because they get too heavy to float — and the picture says nothing about where the water goes after it lands. It corrects the one wrong idea the vivid image could plant, instead of praising the analogy.
5. **The pair works together.** Line 2 explains line 1 rather than repeating it, in language a 10-year-old follows without being talked down to ("too heavy to float", not "condensation exceeds suspension capacity").

**Method note.** Candidates were scored manually against the brief's five criteria in order (the OpenRouter free key was quota-exhausted, so no judge numbers were invented). Runner-up: a queue as a human caterpillar (295 chars) — it lost on criterion 2 (a line of children walking as a caterpillar is familiar to schoolkids, not surprising) and on criterion 4 (it lists behaviours — leaving, joining, cutting in — while the rain entry corrects a real mechanism misconception). The rain entry also teaches something true: rain falls because drops get too heavy, not because something squeezes the cloud.
